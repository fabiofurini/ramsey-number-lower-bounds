02 / The circulant family

Values reported in our study.

We write RC(m,n) for one more than the largest order admitting a circulant colouring with no blue Km and no red Kn. Thus RC(m,n) ≤ R(m,n): an exact circulant value can still be only a lower bound for the ordinary Ramsey number.

Same question, a restricted family.

R(3,7) = 23, whereas RC(3,7) = 22. Our 21-vertex circulant witness proves the lower bound 22. It does not imply that the ordinary Ramsey number is 22.

The tables contain the 26 values in the paper’s circulant study. Six are classical baseline values; our computational procedure establishes the other 20. Eight values, RC(3,n) for n = 13,…,20, are new in this study. The other twelve computational values reproduce earlier results with an explicit reproducible procedure.

Study source: An Integer Programming Approach to Compute Lower Bounds for Ramsey Numbers Using Circulant Graphs, “Computational Study on Circulant Ramsey Numbers”; certificate collection and interpretation ↗. Click a witness to open its graph on a web page. The source certificate is also available to download. The graph certifies the constructive lower-bound side; the matching exclusions are computational results of the study.

The ordinary R(m,n) column gives both lower and upper bounds, or an exact value when known, from Radziszowski’s revision 18, Tables Ia, Ib and IIa. For example, RC(3,13) = 58, while 61 ≤ R(3,13) ≤ 68. See the higher-parameter survey table ↗

What does a witness certify? A graph on v vertices proves existence and the lower bound RC(m,n) ≥ v+1. Equality also requires excluding every larger relevant circulant order; the downloadable graph alone does not prove those exclusions. For example, the 57-vertex witness gives RC(3,13) ≥ 58; the study’s completed exclusions establish RC(3,13) = 58. How both sides are established ↓

03 / How the values were obtained

A witness and the exclusions above it.

For each pair (m,n), a known ordinary Ramsey upper bound U excludes every order at least U. A known circulant witness on L−1 vertices gives a starting lower bound L. We then investigate the remaining orders from the top down.

  1. Encode an order using distance variables.At order v, the distance-space integer program chooses the colours of ⌊v/2⌋ circular-distance classes. Branch-and-cut searches for a colouring avoiding both forbidden clique sizes.
  2. Resolve the clique questions.Target-clique heuristics search for violations; exact clique separation resolves the remaining questions and generates valid cuts. An infeasibility result must come from a completed exact solve.
  3. Inspect orders U−1, U−2, …, L.Each order is either proved infeasible, produces a verified colouring, or remains unresolved at the time limit. The circulant study uses a 24-hour limit and one thread per instance.
  4. Join the two sides of the proof.If a verified colouring is first found at order v after every larger relevant order has been excluded, then v is the largest feasible circulant order and RC(m,n) = v+1. If all tested orders are excluded, the starting witness gives RC(m,n) = L. A timeout leaves the exact value unresolved.

Why one infeasible order is not enough

Circulant feasibility need not be monotone in the graph order. A colouring can fail to exist at one order and exist at a larger one. To establish RC, all larger orders below U must therefore be ruled out; stopping at the first failure would not prove the claimed value.

The downloadable graph certificates independently support the lower-bound side. They do not encode the exhaustive exclusions needed for equality. Those exclusions use the paper’s formulation and completed solver runs; the repository provides the computational procedure.
